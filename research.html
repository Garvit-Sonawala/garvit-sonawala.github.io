MAY 2026 – NOW

Master's Thesis, IIT Gandhinagar

Now

Supervisor: Dr. Rusa Mandal

b → sℓ+ℓ− from underlying theory to measurable observables

My current project focuses on rare flavour-changing decays, particularly the b → sℓ+ℓ− transition. I first worked through the theoretical framework connecting an underlying Lagrangian to the low-energy description of the process: defining the relevant operators, matching the short-distance theory onto the Weak Effective Theory, and obtaining the corresponding Wilson coefficients such as C9 and C10.

The effective theory describes the short-distance quark-level physics, but the quarks themselves are confined inside hadrons. To connect the calculation to a physical process such as B → K*ℓ+ℓ−, the effective operators have to be combined with hadronic matrix elements. These are expressed through form factors and other hadronic quantities, which are obtained using non-perturbative approaches such as lattice QCD and light-cone sum rules, allowing the calculation to be carried through to experimentally measurable observables.

I am now working on the model-dependent side of this calculation: starting from a specified set of new interactions and determining how they modify the Wilson coefficients. I am building a computational pipeline that combines FeynRules, FeynArts, FeynCalc, Package-X and related tools to generate the relevant loop diagrams, construct the amplitudes, reduce the loop integrals, and match the resulting contributions onto the effective operators.

The goal is to make this calculation modular and reusable rather than deriving each new-physics contribution independently by hand. Given a model and its interactions, the pipeline should be able to propagate them through the calculation from the underlying Lagrangian to the corresponding shifts in the Wilson coefficients, and eventually to the predicted rare-decay observables.

Ultimately, the computation serves a physics goal: to determine whether a well-motivated new-physics contribution can modify the relevant Wilson coefficients enough to reduce the discrepancy between theoretical predictions and the observed rare B-decay observables. The project is therefore as much about building a reliable route from theory to prediction as it is about understanding where new physics could enter that chain.

JAN – MAY 2026

Minor Project, Computational Chemistry

IISER Thiruvananthapuram · Supervisor: Dr. R. S. Swathi

I'm majoring in Physics, and I did a minor in chemistry. After finishing the coursework, I did my minor thesis, on a question in computational chemistry: boron atoms form a twelve-atom cluster called B12, a buckled, nearly hexagonal fragment of borophene, and when several B12 units come together into a larger (B12)n cluster, what's the actual lowest-energy arrangement, for n anywhere from 2 to 10?

You can't just minimise your way there with gradient descent, because the energy landscape has a huge number of local minima and a gradient optimiser just falls into whichever one it starts closest to. I used particle swarm optimisation instead, a population of candidate structures that explore the space together and share information about the best position anyone's found, and validated it first on the classical Lennard-Jones cluster problem, where the true global-minimum energies are already published, before trusting it on the real system. That benchmarking step mattered: a plain swarm using a fully connected ("star") communication topology converged fast but collapsed to a 0% success rate by cluster size 6, while a slower, locally connected ("ring") topology held up a bit longer but still failed by the same point. Adding a local optimiser, L-BFGS-B, after every swarm iteration changed the picture completely, pushing the success rate to 100% up to size 8 and holding on partially as far as 15.

The physical part of the problem is that B12 isn't a normal van der Waals cluster. It's a 4π-electron system, so by the Hückel rule it's antiaromatic, and stacking two antiaromatic rings face to face carries a real energy penalty that a standard dispersion potential simply doesn't know about. I used an improved Lennard-Jones potential with an explicit orientation-dependent antiaromatic correction on top, and ran the whole thing twice, once with the correction and once without, specifically to isolate its effect. That correction turns out to be worth 10 to 20 kJ/mol, large enough to rule out the face-to-face stacked geometries a plain dispersion model would otherwise favour as the global minimum.

Treating each 12-atom monomer as a rigid body, rather than optimising all 36 atomic coordinates per monomer independently, cut the search space for a ten-monomer cluster from 360 dimensions down to 54, which is what actually made the larger cluster sizes tractable on the HPC cluster at IISER. The results matched an independent evolutionary-algorithm reference closely for the smaller clusters, and picked out n = 6 as a genuine magic-number cluster, meaning it's anomalously stable compared to its neighbours, a result all three methods agreed on independently.

Two things stuck with me from this project: test your method on a problem where you already know the answer before trusting it on something new, and never add a correction to your model without checking what changes when you turn it off. Both are habits I try to carry into everything I build.

MAY – JUL 2025

Research Intern, TIFR Mumbai

Supervisor: Dr. Achamveedu Gopakumar

Pulsar timing arrays are one of the strangest gravitational-wave detectors around: instead of an interferometer, you use millisecond pulsars themselves as cosmic clocks, spread across the galaxy, and watch for a correlated deviation in their arrival times, called a timing residual, that a slowly varying gravitational wave from a supermassive black-hole binary would leave behind. The signal you're looking for sits well below the noise floor for a huge fraction of realistic sources, which is what made this a machine learning problem as much as a physics one.

I started by simulating twelve years of timing residuals for three real millisecond pulsars, J1909−3744, J1713+0747 and J0437−4715, injected with realistic Gaussian timing noise, using the 0PN quadrupole-radiation waveform for a circular supermassive black-hole binary, later extended to 2PN, which adds relativistic corrections the Newtonian approximation misses. That gave me a controlled dataset where I actually knew the true chirp mass and could dial the signal-to-noise ratio up and down to see where each method broke.

The first model is a 1D convolutional network that just answers one question: is there a genuine signal buried in this residual, or is it noise. It's a fairly standard architecture, three convolution-batchnorm-ReLU-pooling blocks feeding into a dense classifier, but tuning it against a synthetic dataset where you control the SNR directly is what let me actually characterise its failure mode properly: close to 98% accuracy once the SNR is around 18, degrading smoothly down to about 65% by SNR 1, rather than a hard, misleading cliff.

The second model is a small Elman recurrent network trained to regress the chirp mass directly from the residual sequence, using mean relative error rather than raw MSE as the metric that actually matters physically. That one goes from roughly 11% error at SNR 4.5 down to about 2.4% at SNR 20, which is the kind of curve you want: useful even in a fairly noisy regime, and genuinely precise once the signal is clean. A Bayesian version of the same estimator adds calibrated uncertainty on top, so a low-confidence chirp-mass estimate actually reads as low-confidence instead of a silently wrong number.

None of this replaces a real PTA pipeline, obviously, real detections come from correlating dozens of pulsars over decades, not three over twelve years. But it was a genuine, hands-on introduction to why the field is leaning so heavily on machine learning right now: PTA datasets are large, the signals are weak and drowned in red noise, and a well-characterised neural estimator is often faster and more honest about its own uncertainty than a brute-force matched-filter search over an enormous parameter space.

JUL – SEP 2024

Research Intern, IIT Guwahati

Supervisor: Dr. Subhaditya Bhattacharya

Dark matter makes up about 85% of the matter in the universe, and we've never directly detected a single particle of it. Almost everything we know comes from its gravitational footprint: galaxy rotation curves that stay flat far beyond where the visible matter runs out, gravitational lensing around galaxy clusters, the way the hot gas and the mass visibly separated in the Bullet Cluster after two clusters collided, and the specific pattern of anisotropies in the cosmic microwave background, which only comes out right if you include a cold dark matter component in the model. None of these tell you what dark matter actually is, but together they pin down how much of it there has to be, and that number is called the relic abundance.

This project was about deriving where that number comes from, from first principles, rather than quoting it. The starting point is the Boltzmann equation, which describes how the phase space distribution of a particle species evolves in time, but this isn't a fixed background: you have to build it on an expanding FLRW spacetime, work out the relevant Christoffel symbols for that metric, and substitute them back into Liouville's equation before it says anything useful about number density. That gives an equation for how a particle species's number density changes under two competing effects: dilution from the expansion of the universe, and creation or destruction through actual particle interactions, the annihilation and production processes captured in the collision term.

The physically useful move is to stop tracking the number density itself and instead track the comoving number density, or yield, Y = n/s, where s is the entropy density, because that quantity stays flat except when something is actually happening. In the radiation-dominated era, converting the time variable to x = m/T, where T is the temperature, gives a single differential equation for how Y evolves, and it has a clean interpretation once you get there: while the annihilation rate is much faster than the Hubble expansion rate, Y tracks its equilibrium value closely; once the universe expands and cools enough that the interaction rate drops below the expansion rate, the species decouples, or freezes out, and Y flattens out at whatever value it had at that moment. That frozen-in value is the relic abundance.

It's a satisfying derivation because every approximation in it is motivated by something physical rather than just algebraic convenience, and because the same freeze-out logic, tune the annihilation cross-section and you shift where and how much freezes out, is the whole reason weakly interacting massive particles became such a popular dark matter candidate in the first place: get the cross-section roughly at the weak scale, and the freeze-out calculation spits out almost exactly the observed dark matter density, unprompted. I presented this work as a poster at ANVESHA 2024.
